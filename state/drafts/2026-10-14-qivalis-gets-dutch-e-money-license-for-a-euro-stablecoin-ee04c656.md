The Dutch central bank has granted Qivalis an electronic money institution license, putting the 37-bank consortium one step away from issuing a euro-denominated stablecoin under the EU's MiCA rules.

De Nederlandsche Bank's approval, announced Wednesday, subjects Qivalis to ongoing supervision and makes it eligible to issue electronic money tokens (EMTs). The Amsterdam-based company still has homework first. It must publish a white paper and meet the remaining MiCA requirements before launch, which it says will happen in the coming months. Distribution would run through banks, crypto exchanges, and payment providers.

## The pitch

Qivalis is a bank consortium, and a big one. It counts BBVA, BNP Paribas, and ING among its backers, and it added 25 banks across 15 countries in May to reach 37 institutions total. The idea is to give European banks and payment companies a way to move euros onchain, under European law.

CEO Jan-Oliver Sell framed it as a sovereignty play. "We believe Europe should have a euro-denominated on-chain settlement asset issued under European law and subject to European regulatory standards," he said.

The market context explains the framing. Stablecoins are overwhelmingly a dollar business. Tether's USDT alone has a $183 billion market cap, more than 60% of the $303 billion total, according to DefiLlama data cited by Cointelegraph. Euro tokens are fighting for what is left.

## The catch

Two things stand out to me.

First, the product. Under MiCA, EMT holders can redeem tokens at face value at any time, but the tokens cannot pay interest. That is fine design for settlement plumbing. It is a harder sell as something to actually hold, especially against dollar tokens with a decade of liquidity behind them.

Second, the demand. CoinDesk notes Qivalis has not said which member banks will use the token, or how much money they expect to move through it. A 37-bank announcement with zero committed users is a familiar shape in crypto. The logo wall is impressive. The order book is empty.

My read: the license is real, and the regulatory path is real. MiCA has done what Brussels wanted, which is give banks a compliant way to issue tokens on European terms. What regulation cannot do is manufacture a reason for anyone to hold a euro token that pays nothing, in a market where the dollar side already won the liquidity war. Maybe bank-to-bank settlement is enough of a business. Nobody knows, and right now Qivalis is not saying.
