At least $247 million of the $6.3 billion stolen from Binance last month has been pushed through bitcoin mixing transactions designed to hide where the money goes, according to Elliptic founder Tom Robinson, who laid out the firm's findings on LinkedIn Monday.

The transactions are coinjoins, which pool bitcoin from multiple users so the trail breaks apart. One detail stands out. Elliptic found that nearly all of the mixing was coordinated by a single entity known only as Kruw, and that the activity is "increasingly falling within a core set of daily hours," as Robinson put it.

Read that again. The laundering runs on a schedule.

## A shift pattern for stolen money

Robinson said the timing could mean the funds have been handed off to China-based money launderers, a pattern seen in previous North Korean thefts. He was careful to call the timing inconclusive. I appreciate the honesty, but the shape of this is familiar. North Korea steals the coins, then hands them to outside launderers, as it has in past heists. The fixed hours suggest a handoff to people who treat this as a job, because for them it is one.

The FBI has attributed the Oct. 29 attack to TraderTraitor, a North Korean hacking group. North Korea denies involvement. Binance's own account of the breach is the part that should worry every company holding crypto. The exchange said the heist began with a single job interview, after which hackers spent months inside its systems using compromised software and fake recruitment interviews.

One interview. That was the door.

## Most of the money is going nowhere

Here is the number that matters more than the $247 million. Elliptic says about $2.35 billion was still sitting in wallets tied to the theft as of Nov. 20, with little evidence any of it had been converted to cash. Another $2.85 billion has been frozen or seized, an effort backed by a worldwide freezing order from an Abu Dhabi court.

The rough scoreboard: $2.85 billion frozen or seized, $2.35 billion parked and untouched, $247 million in the wash. Elliptic says most of the stolen funds were probably still controlled by the North Korean hackers. But controlling bitcoin and spending it are different problems. Every coin that moves gets watched. Every coin that sits still is a target for the next freezing order.

This is the part of big crypto thefts that doesn't get said enough. The theft is the easy half. North Korea's hackers are genuinely good at breaking in. Turning $6.3 billion in traceable bitcoin into usable money, with every blockchain analytics firm on earth staring at the wallets, is a slower and worse problem. The $247 million through Kruw's coinjoins is the sound of them testing the pipes.

Whether the pipes hold is another question. Coinjoins make tracing harder, not impossible, and Elliptic is clearly watching the schedule. Nobody knows how much of the remaining $2.35 billion ever moves, let alone cashes out. My guess is the answer arrives in fixed daily hours.
