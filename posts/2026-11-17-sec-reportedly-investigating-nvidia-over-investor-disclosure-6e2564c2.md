The Securities and Exchange Commission is opening a civil probe into Nvidia over whether the company's disclosures misled investors, [TechCrunch reports](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/17/sec-reportedly-investigating-nvidia-amid-huang-exit/), citing The Wall Street Journal. Details on the probe itself are thin. What Nvidia said, or failed to say, and when, isn't public yet. But the investigation lands on a company already deep in its own mess.

## How we got here

Jensen Huang resigned as CEO after news broke of his extramarital affairs, including one with Alice Huang, an executive at Singapore-based data company Megaspeed who is alleged to be a Chinese spy. Read that sentence again. The CEO of Nvidia was allegedly involved with an executive at a firm the Commerce Department has been investigating since late 2024, on suspicion it diverted Nvidia chips to China in violation of US export laws.

Congress has demanded Huang answer questions about the affair and whether it influenced his stance that export restrictions should be loosened. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent was blunter over the weekend, accusing Nvidia of knowingly selling powerful chips to China to "goose their revenues and their earnings."

## The earnings split screen

All of this broke as Nvidia reported earnings. Revenue came in at $115.8 billion, up 103% from a year ago. The stock has fallen more than 10% since Huang departed.

That split is the story. Demand for Nvidia's chips is not the problem. Revenue doubling year over year tells you the AI buildout is still hungry. The problem is governance, and the market is now pricing the possibility that what Nvidia told investors about its own house was incomplete.

## What the SEC is really asking

I keep coming back to the disclosure question, because it's the sharp edge here. Public companies have to tell investors about material risks. If Nvidia's leadership knew the CEO was personally entangled with an executive at a company under federal investigation for diverting its chips, and knew it while the CEO was publicly arguing for looser export rules, shareholders had a right to hear about it. Whether staying quiet crosses the legal line into misleading disclosures is exactly what the SEC will now decide.

Bessent's comment deserves a second look too. A sitting Treasury Secretary accusing Nvidia of knowingly selling chips to China to pad earnings is not routine Washington noise. If the government actually believes that, the SEC probe may be the smallest of Nvidia's problems. If it's bluster, it's still an extraordinary thing to say out loud.

Nobody knows how this plays out. The probe is civil, not criminal, and "reportedly" is doing real work in every headline about it. But a stock down more than 10% on the same day the company reports revenue up 103% tells you investors have stopped giving Nvidia the benefit of the doubt.
