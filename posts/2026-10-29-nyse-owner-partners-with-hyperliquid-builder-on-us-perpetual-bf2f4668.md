Intercontinental Exchange, the company that owns the New York Stock Exchange, plans to bring perpetual futures to U.S. traders through a partnership with Trade[XYZ], the largest builder of markets on the decentralized exchange Hyperliquid. The venue, branded ICE[XYZ], needs CFTC approval before it lists anything.

Perpetual futures are contracts that let traders bet on whether an asset's price will rise or fall, without the contract ever expiring. They're heavily traded in crypto, but mostly on offshore venues that U.S. investors can't use. ICE[XYZ] would list perps on equities, indices, commodities, FX and crypto, offered through participating brokers and platforms, with new exchange and clearing infrastructure built for the purpose, according to CoinDesk's report on the announcement.

## The numbers behind the deal

Trade[XYZ]'s existing markets on Hyperliquid have topped $650 billion in volume in just over a year, per the companies' press release. Those markets will keep running alongside the U.S. venue. ICE is also making a strategic investment in Trade[XYZ], though neither side disclosed terms.

Timing, product specifics and eligibility are all still unannounced. Trade[XYZ] said it will share details as approvals progress, which is a polite way of saying the CFTC holds the calendar here.

## My read

This is the most interesting crypto market structure story in a while, and I don't think that's close. Perps trade heavily in crypto, and for years U.S. investors simply couldn't access the offshore venues where that trading happens. The owner of the NYSE deciding there's a regulated business here tells you how big the demand signal got.

There's also a quiet irony worth sitting with. Hyperliquid is a decentralized exchange, the kind of thing built partly to route around gatekeepers. Now its biggest market builder is the pipeline for a broker-intermediated, ICE-cleared product, assuming Washington signs off. The DeFi-to-Wall-Street round trip keeps getting shorter.

One detail from the CoinDesk piece stuck with me. ICE already has a bitcoin-focused subsidiary, Bakkt, and per CoinDesk, CEO Jeffrey Sprecher mentioned it only once on Thursday's earnings call, with Bakkt otherwise not coming up. When the parent company goes outside its own crypto arm for a deal like this, that says something about how the first wave of institutional crypto plays aged.

The open question is approval. "Subject to CFTC approval" is doing a lot of work in this announcement. Nobody knows the timeline, and ICE isn't pretending to. If it clears, U.S. perps on everything from stocks to bitcoin become a real market, and the offshore venues lose the regulatory moat that kept American money out.
