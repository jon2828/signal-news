HSBC launched its first stablecoin on Friday, a Hong Kong dollar token called RedCoin (RCHKD), and it's going straight into PayMe, the payments app more than 3.3 million people in Hong Kong already use.

PayMe users will be able to activate a stablecoin wallet inside the app over the coming days. Once they do, they can send money to friends and family and pay at more than 100,000 merchants, according to the bank.

## The details

HSBC says RedCoin will be fully backed by high-quality liquid assets held in separate accounts. The bank plans to add a RedCoin wallet to its main Hong Kong mobile banking app later this year, and customers will eventually be able to use the token to subscribe to tokenized investment products, including a new share class of HSBC's existing Hong Kong dollar money market fund.

John O'Neill, HSBC's managing director for digital assets and currencies, said the bank will "soon add more products, including expanding into wholesale corporate and institutional use cases."

Maggie Ng, HSBC's CEO for Hong Kong, framed it bigger. Money is moving from traditional infrastructure to digital networks, she said, and assets and information are moving with it. She described those networks as always on, and said they are rich in data and increasingly programmable.

## The license behind it

This didn't come out of nowhere. Hong Kong's Stablecoins Ordinance took effect in August 2025, creating a licensing regime for fiat-referenced stablecoin issuers. In April 2026, the Hong Kong Monetary Authority issued its first stablecoin licenses to HSBC and Anchorpoint Financial, a joint venture formed by Standard Chartered and HKT together with Animoca Brands. Anchorpoint began a phased institutional rollout of its own HKD token, HKDAP, in August, focused on payments and settlement.

The two licensed issuers have now split the market cleanly. Anchorpoint went institutional first. HSBC went retail first, and it went retail through an app people already have on their phones.

## Why this one matters

I've watched banks talk about tokenized money for years, and most of it stays in press releases and pilots. This is different in one specific way: distribution. HSBC is one of three commercial banks authorized to issue HKD banknotes, and it's putting a tokenized version of that money into an app with millions of users and a merchant network already built.

Vincent Chok, CEO of First Digital (the issuer behind FDUSD), made the core point in emailed comments to CoinDesk: "Integrating stablecoins into platforms users already rely on removes the need for a separate wallet or exchange account." He added that everyday exposure "builds familiarity and confidence in stablecoins across the wider market."

He's right, and that's also the part worth watching with some suspicion. A bank-issued, fully backed, licensed stablecoin inside a consumer payments app is about as far from crypto's self-custody ethos as you can get. RedCoin is programmable money issued by one of the largest banks on earth, under a government licensing regime. It is probably useful. It is not neutral infrastructure, not even slightly.

HSBC has been building toward this for a while. It runs HSBC Orion for digital bonds, sells a Gold Token to retail clients, offers a Tokenised Deposit Service for wholesale payments, and has taken part in the HKMA's e-HKD pilot and Project Ensemble. RedCoin is the first piece aimed squarely at everyday spending.

Whether PayMe users actually want to pay in a stablecoin, when the app already moves Hong Kong dollars fine, is an open question. My guess is most people won't notice a difference, which is exactly the point. The token either disappears into the plumbing and works, or it becomes a feature nobody asked for. Nobody knows how this plays out.
