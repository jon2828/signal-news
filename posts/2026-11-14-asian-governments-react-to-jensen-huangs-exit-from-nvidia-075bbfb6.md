Jensen Huang is out at Nvidia, and the past week of reactions from Asian governments is a decent map of how much of the AI world runs through one company.

Quick recap of how we got here. On November 8, Nvidia's board opened an independent investigation after reports that Huang had been targeted by a Chinese spy operation. Huang admitted to an affair with Alice Huang, a former **executive** at Singapore‑based **chip** company Megaspeed. TechCrunch pulled together the government responses, and they split cleanly into two camps: countries protecting supply deals, and countries with open investigations.

## China denies the spy story

China's foreign ministry rejected the allegations on November 10, calling them "groundless" and a smear against Beijing. On Friday a spokesperson went further, saying the ministry was not aware of reports linking restricted Nvidia chips to a Shanghai data centre associated with Alice Huang, and reiterating Beijing's standing opposition to U.S. export controls.

The denial is doing a lot of work at once. Underneath it sits a real problem: more than **$3 billion** in Chinese AI‑computing projects rely on Nvidia's restricted processors, according to Reuters, and those chips are already hard to obtain under U.S. export rules. Nvidia spent years building close ties with partners across Asia, and Huang was the face of those ties. Whoever runs the company next inherits that relationship, or doesn't.

## Singapore's investigation is the messy part

Singapore has been on this case longer than anyone. Authorities seized Alice Huang's passport in June 2025 as part of an investigation into possible false declarations about server purchases, according to the Ministry of Home Affairs. The question was whether servers shipped from Singapore to Malaysia were later sent on to other countries without proper declaration.

In February 2026, police concluded there was insufficient evidence for the false‑declaration offences. But the ministry said she had knowingly provided inaccurate information about her role at Megaspeed. She was issued a warning, barred from re‑entering Singapore, and got her passport back that month.

Two details stand out. Singapore says it received limited assistance from U.S. authorities despite repeated requests for information about U.S. server maker Supermicro, which is a pointed thing to say publicly. And the ministry's investigation into Megaspeed and six other individuals is still ongoing. It did not name them. This story is not finished.

## South Korea, Taiwan, and Japan want reassurance

South Korea gave the most concrete response. The Science Ministry said Nvidia reaffirmed its plan to supply **260,000** advanced GPUs to the country and to continue joint AI projects. Samsung and SK Hynix supply the memory that goes into Nvidia's AI processors, so Seoul has money on both sides of the table. The government's AI‑strategy council still held an emergency meeting on Friday to assess the fallout, per local media.

Taiwan's economy minister Kung Ming‑hsin said the departure was unlikely to significantly change the **growth** of the global AI industry. Taipei's city government said Nvidia's planned overseas headquarters in the city remains on track, and that the company has met its contractual obligations "so far." That qualifier is doing quiet work.

Japan's Industry Ministry said it would closely monitor Nvidia, describing the chipmaker as an "important player" in Japan's AI‑semiconductor industry, per Reuters. Deputy chief cabinet secretary Masanao Ozaki said it was too early to assess the impact and stressed Japan's commitment to a stable semiconductor supply.

## What I make of it

The pattern is hard to miss. The governments with GPU allocations and construction projects on the line, South Korea, Taiwan, Japan, rushed to say nothing changes. The governments with investigations, Singapore and China, are tangled in a case that predates Huang's exit and will outlast it.

What nobody has answered is the actual question: who runs Nvidia now, and does the board's investigation find anything beyond the affair? Until that clears up, every one of these reassurances is provisional. The 260,000 GPUs, the Taipei headquarters, the $3 billion in Chinese projects, all of it waits on whatever Nvidia's board does next.
