The Clarity Act failed a procedural vote in the Senate on Sept. 15, drawing 49 votes in favor and 50 against, well short of the 60 needed to advance. The bill was the industry's best shot at a lasting U.S. rulebook for digital assets, one that would have settled which tokens fall under the SEC and which under the CFTC. Instead, [CoinDesk reports](https://www.coindesk.com/business/2026/10/04/the-clarity-act-stalled-crypto-dealmakers-aren-t-hitting-the-brakes-yet), negotiations foundered over ethics restrictions on senior officials' crypto business interests, including President Trump's, alongside concerns about investor protection and illicit finance.

With the November midterms approaching and little legislative time left, the defeat sharply cuts the odds of passage this year. Regulators keep filling the gap, which is precisely the outcome the industry spent years saying it couldn't live with.

## The deals keep coming

You'd expect a stalled market structure bill to cool crypto M&A. It hasn't. CoinDesk reports that crypto M&A has hit record levels, and the bankers in its reporting aren't hitting the brakes.

That gap between Washington gridlock and deal flow is the story. The bill's champions, Sen. Cynthia Lummis among them, spent years arguing that a clear rulebook would give businesses and investors the certainty they needed. The Senate failed to deliver it, and the money showed up anyway.

There are dissenters on both sides. A companion CoinDesk video has Patrick McHenry arguing there's still a lame-duck window for the bill. Another has New York's crypto regulator saying nothing changes after the Clarity Act. Both can be true at once, which tells you how unsettled the ground is.

## The clarity argument has a problem

For years the industry's core pitch was simple: give us clear rules and the capital will flow. The Clarity Act's failure is a live test of that claim. The rules didn't arrive. The capital is moving regardless.

Two readings. The charitable one is that dealmakers have learned to price regulatory risk the way they price any other risk, and the SEC-versus-CFTC question matters less inside a deal than it does in a lobbying deck. The less charitable one is that clarity was always partly a political ask, and the real cost of not having it is lower than the industry said.

I keep coming back to how the bill died. Not over the technical core, the agency split. It stalled partly over ethics rules aimed at officials' own crypto interests, including the president's. The politics of crypto in Washington keep getting tangled in the personal finances of the people writing the rules. Until that changes, I wouldn't bet on any market structure bill passing, no matter how friendly the environment looks on paper.

Whether record dealmaking survives another year of regulatory limbo is a real question. For now the market's answer to Washington's gridlock is to keep signing.
