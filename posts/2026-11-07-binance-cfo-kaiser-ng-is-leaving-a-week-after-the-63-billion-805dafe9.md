Binance's chief financial officer, Kaiser Ng, is leaving the exchange, barely a week after hackers stole $6.3 billion worth of cryptocurrency from its users, [TechCrunch reports](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/07/binance-cfo-is-leaving-to-pursue-a-new-venture-after-6-3-billion-hack/).

According to Bloomberg, co-CEO Yi He said in a statement that Ng's departure will let him "pursue a new venture after more than five years at Binance," and that a transition plan has been "implemented over the last half year." Justin Yek will replace him as CFO.

## The official story, and the timing problem

Take the statement at face value and this is routine. A transition planned for six months predates the hack by a long stretch. Executives leave. Companies have succession plans. Fine.

I keep coming back to the optics anyway. Your CFO walks days after a $6.3 billion theft, and the explanation is a quote so polished it could be stapled to any departure at any company. "Pursue a new venture" is what you say when there is nothing else to say. Maybe the timeline is exactly as described and the announcement just landed badly. Maybe the hack moved it up. Nobody outside Binance knows, and Binance is not going to tell us.

## The numbers that matter more

The CFO shuffle is a sideshow next to what users did with their money. Binance froze withdrawals for several days after the hack. When accounts were unfrozen, users pulled a net $3.6 billion in the first few hours. That is a bank run in miniature. People did not wait around to find out whether Binance was fine. They got out first and planned to ask questions later, which tells you how much trust was left.

Binance and the FBI have blamed a North Korean group called TraderTraitor for the theft. The company has also promised to absorb the loss itself, saying "Binance will bear the financial impact of the stolen funds," and that the losses will not be passed on to users.

That is the right thing to say, and saying it is the easy part. A $6.3 billion commitment is a serious test of any balance sheet, and Binance's books are not something outsiders can check in real time. If the company makes every user whole, it will have earned back some of the trust that walked out the door. Until then, the promise is just a promise.

What I am watching: whether the outflows stop at $3.6 billion or keep going, and whether Justin Yek says anything about the state of the finances once he sits down. New CFOs who inherit a mess tend to go quiet. If he does, that will be its own answer.
