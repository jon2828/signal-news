# Nvidia stops selling H200 chips in China after Huang's exit

**Date:** 2026-11-17

Nvidia will no longer sell its H200 chips in China. Acting CEO Colette Kress announced this on Tuesday during the company's first earnings call since Jensen Huang stepped down last week. The decision ends Nvidia's participation in a controversial loophole in the U.S. ban on advanced chips to China.

The H200 arrangement was never standard policy. Huang negotiated the deal personally with President Donald Trump, and the chips had to be shipped through the United States so the government could collect a 25 % tariff. China hawks criticized the loophole. In April, Huang told podcaster Dwarkesh Patel that "to concede that market for the United States technology industry is a disservice to our country."

## The exit and the cleanup

Huang's ouster - reportedly linked in part to an affair with the CEO of a company under investigation for smuggling chips into China - has given the board room an opportunity to change course. On the same day Nvidia announced the H200 decision, the company said it is creating a board‑level committee to oversee national‑security policy, export‑control compliance, and global‑trade risk.

The timing also coincides with new rules issued Monday that bar advanced U.S. chips from foreign data centers that serve Chinese customers remotely. The previous workaround of buying chips for an overseas facility and renting compute back to Chinese labs is now closed.

## What it costs

Jefferies estimates the new export rules could trim **$44 billion to $62 billion** from Nvidia's data‑center revenue next year. Kress responded that the backlog of demand for next‑generation chips such as Vera Rubin means the rules will have no impact on top‑line revenue.

Critics have claimed that up to **20 %** of Nvidia's revenue is linked to demand from China. The company's Singapore subsidiary reported **$104 billion** in annual revenue in 2025 before Nvidia stopped disclosing those figures. If the China‑linked portion is that large, the "no impact" claim is debatable.

In the longer term, Nvidia could lose potential customers in Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, and Indonesia.

## The awkward part for Beijing

China has largely refused deliveries of the H200, preferring to promote its domestic chip industry even though many AI compute users still desire Nvidia's equipment, according to Jacob Feldgoise, a researcher at Georgetown's Center for Security and Emerging Technology.

All of China's leading AI models show evidence of having been trained on Nvidia Grace Blackwell chips, which are prohibited from export to China. Export‑control attorney Dan Fisher‑Owens told TechCrunch that new rules will require large data centers to certify they are not serving any Chinese customers. Facilities that cannot provide such certification may turn to alternative chip providers, potentially reducing Nvidia's market share.

Whether the rules will effectively stop Chinese labs from training on Blackwell‑class hardware, or simply add administrative overhead, remains uncertain.
