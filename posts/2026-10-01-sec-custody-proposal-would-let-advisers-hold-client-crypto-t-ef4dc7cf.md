The SEC proposed a new custody framework for crypto assets on Thursday, one that would let investment advisers and regulated funds hold client crypto themselves when no qualified custodian will take it.

The proposal runs 760 pages, per CoinDesk, and it does the expected plumbing work: defining which companies can properly custody crypto, and what records and federal disclosures advisers and funds owe. It would also let state trust companies act as custodians, per The Block. SEC Chairman Paul Atkins pitched it as a fix for rules that predate the internet, promising a "compliant pathway where none existed before."

## The self-custody carve-out

The interesting part is the self-custody provision. If an adviser determines that no permitted custodian is available for a given asset, the adviser can custody it in-house. That is a narrow door. CoinDesk notes it would likely remain an unusual circumstance once the rule broadens who counts as a custodian. But the door exists, and it matters for asset managers and hedge funds that want to hold bitcoin directly rather than through an ETF, as The Block points out.

The terminology needs untangling, and Commissioner Hester Peirce did the untangling herself. In crypto, self-custody means you hold your own keys. In this proposal it means your asset manager holds them for you. Peirce used her statement to argue for the real thing: "True self-custody is not the right choice for everyone, but many crypto owners prize being able to custody their own assets." Then a shrug: "But I digress."

I appreciate the candor. I also think the naming choice is going to confuse people. A rule that redefines self-custody as "your adviser custodies" invites misreading, and plenty of commentary will oblige.

## Congress failed, so the agencies are writing the rules

The context matters. The Clarity Act failed to pass the Senate, and the SEC and CFTC have been moving by rulemaking instead, per The Block. The SEC released its innovation exemption last month and its Reg Crypto release in August, per CoinDesk, and the CFTC has filed its own crypto rulemaking with the White House. Atkins says more proposals are on the horizon.

Nate Geraci, president of NovaDius, put the politics bluntly on X: "Some politicians are going to wish they passed the Clarity Act." He is probably right. Statutes are slow to pass and hard to repeal. Agency rules are quick to write and quick to rewrite, which works for crypto today and could work against it under a different commission. Nobody knows how that plays out, but it is the trade the industry made when it cheered rulemaking over legislation.

This is also Peirce's last week. CoinDesk calls the proposal a swan song for the inaugural Crypto Task Force chief, which gives the timing a certain symmetry: she exits with the agency mid-stream on a run of crypto rulemakings.

None of this is final. The comment period runs 60 days, per The Block, and the fight over who qualifies as a custodian starts now. Watch that definition. It decides who gets the business.
