The CFTC will not write a separate rulebook for Hyperliquid or any other onchain trading platform. Chairman Michael Selig said Thursday that crypto exchanges can offer perpetual futures to U.S. retail traders if they obtain regulatory approval or partner with a licensed exchange, and that blockchain-based platforms face the same requirements as traditional derivatives venues.

"We're not going to treat you differently and say because it's onchain, you have a different set of requirements," Selig said at the Capitol Crypto Forum in Washington, hosted by The Hill and The Block.

## The door is open, but it is the same door

Selig pointed to Backpack as the model. The crypto exchange secured approval this week to operate a U.S. derivatives exchange and clearinghouse, along with registration as a futures broker.

Hyperliquid itself remains unavailable to U.S. customers. But firms are already building regulated routes to its market. Kraken parent Payward announced plans in September to offer perpetual futures through the CFTC-regulated Bitnomial Exchange using Hyperliquid's blockchain, subject to approval.

Selig also argued that federal oversight could keep crypto trading from moving offshore, and he defended stock-linked perpetual futures, which face a legal challenge over which agency should regulate them.

## My take

This is a "come inside the tent" speech, and a reasonably clear one. No exemptions. No lighter touch because your matching engine is a smart contract. If you want U.S. retail traders, you register and you play by the existing book.

I keep coming back to the irony for Hyperliquid specifically. The pitch for onchain perps was disintermediation. The U.S. version now taking shape runs through Bitnomial and Kraken's parent company. Decentralized exchange, fully intermediated distribution. That may be the only viable path for years, and it is worth being honest about what it is: a wrapper, not a revolution.

The offshore argument is Selig's strongest card. A licensing path that crypto-native firms can actually navigate could pull some trading back onshore. Backpack got through, which suggests the path exists. But one approval is a precedent, not a trend, and plenty of firms will look at the compliance bill and stay put.

The stock-linked perps fight is the sleeper here. Selig is defending products whose regulatory home is being contested in court. If that challenge goes against the CFTC, the "we can handle this" posture gets messier fast. Nobody knows how that plays out.
