The US Treasury Department has withdrawn a controversial 2020 proposal that would have forced financial institutions to collect information on crypto transactions of $250 or more.

On Friday, the Treasury's Financial Crimes Enforcement Network and the Federal Reserve Board of Governors rescinded the "Travel Rule," which would have dropped the threshold for collecting transaction information from $3,000 down to $250. That change would have swept a huge share of ordinary crypto transfers into a reporting regime built for large ones.

## The official reason

FinCEN's stated reasons are bureaucratic, not philosophical. More than five years had passed since the proposal. The convertible virtual currency industry had changed. Fresh public input would help. "The Agencies believe that any future, potential amendments to the Recordkeeping Rule and Travel Rule related to CVC should be considered in light of these recent developments to ensure that such amendments are fit for purpose," the filing said.

Read that again. They did not say the rule was a bad idea. They said it was an old idea.

## What the rule would have done

When FinCEN proposed the threshold cut, it argued that bad actors were increasingly using smaller transactions to move illicit funds. Lower the bar to $250, the logic went, and you catch more of them.

Coin Center saw it differently. The group warned that dropping the threshold from $3,000 to $250 would subject far more ordinary transactions to what it called "warrantless financial surveillance." That framing matters. A $3,000 threshold targets sizable transfers. A $250 threshold is a dragnet pointed at everyone.

## The reaction

Coin Center said in a blog post Friday that it was glad to see the proposal rescinded, and pointed out this is part of a pattern. Last month Treasury withdrew the proposed "unhosted wallet" rule and the cryptocurrency mixing rules, both of which Coin Center had also opposed. "Taken together, these withdrawals close the books on several long-pending proposals that would have expanded the Bank Secrecy Act's already extensive financial surveillance regime," the organization said.

Ji Hun Kim, CEO of the Crypto Council for Innovation, called the withdrawal the "right call." "It had not moved forward over the past several years for good reason," he said, adding that lowering the threshold for cross-border transfers "would have placed undue burden on institutions."

## My read

This is a real win. I'll take it. The $250 rule was one of those proposals that sounds modest in a press release and looks enormous in practice, and the fact that it sat untouched for five years suggests even its authors lost interest in defending it.

But keep the champagne corked. FinCEN withdrew the rule because the paperwork was stale, not because anyone at Treasury decided that warrantless surveillance of small transactions is wrong. The original rationale, that criminals route illicit money through small transfers, was never retracted. And the filing's own language leaves the door wide open: future amendments should be "fit for purpose." A rewritten version, drafted for the 2026 industry instead of the 2020 one, could appear whenever Treasury feels like it.

The 2020 surveillance agenda is being dismantled piece by piece. What replaces it is the question nobody has answered yet.
