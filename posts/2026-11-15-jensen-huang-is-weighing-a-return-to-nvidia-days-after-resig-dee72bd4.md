# Jensen Huang is weighing a return to Nvidia days after resigning

**Date:** 2026-11-15

Jensen Huang is reportedly discussing a return to the Nvidia CEO role, days after resigning over reports of alleged extramarital affairs, one involving a woman that U.S. intelligence officials suspect has ties to Chinese intelligence. The Financial Times broke the story, citing multiple people familiar with the matter, as reported by Cointelegraph.

One source told the FT that a "war room" of Silicon Valley executives is being assembled to lobby for Huang's return, and that the group wants White House support. The source added, "Everyone that matters in tech outside the frontier labs loves Jensen."

### Public campaign

A public petition called **"Bring Jensen Back"** claims more than 161,000 signatures, according to its organizers, who list Elon Musk and Epic Games CEO Tim Sweeney among its supporters. The campaign website also says it has over 9,000 signatures from current and former Nvidia employees.

## The pushback

The comeback effort runs into political resistance. Treasury Secretary **Scott Bessent** said on Sunday that Nvidia needs a "dramatic shake‑up" and should hire a CEO from outside the company. President **Donald Trump** suggested on Thursday that former Apple CEO **Tim Cook** take the job.

The original problems have not disappeared. An independent board investigation is still under way, and scrutiny of Nvidia's chip sales to China continues, with questions about compliance with U.S. export restrictions involving customers in Asia that may have ties to China. Reuters has also reported a third alleged affair.

Investors remain uneasy. Nvidia shares fell as much as 14% on Thursday before recovering to close 4.3% lower.

## Analysis

The speed of the response is notable. Huang resigned amid a scandal that touches on national‑security concerns, yet within days a lobbying effort and a large petition have emerged. The argument that "everyone that matters loves Jensen" is more a show of force than a substantive defense.

The petition numbers are self‑reported. The 161,000 figure comes from the organizers, and the 9,000 employee count is taken from the campaign's own site. No independent verification is available, so the figures should be treated as unconfirmed until corroborated.

The national‑security issue does not hinge on public signatures. If concerns about Huang's judgment were serious enough to force his exit, a lobbying campaign does not resolve them. The board investigation exists precisely because this is not merely a PR problem, and it has not yet concluded.

The White House angle cuts both ways. The war room seeks Trump's backing, yet Trump is publicly floating Tim Cook, while Bessent wants an outsider. The campaign's most vocal supporters are therefore offering conflicting messages.

What is clear is that personal popularity does not address the underlying security and governance questions. The board will have to balance Huang's legacy against an ongoing investigation and political pressure.
